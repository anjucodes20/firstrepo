Classification & Anatomy

The Fundamental Types of Colours

In art and science, colors are categorized by how they are mixed, their temperature, their saturation, and their optical characteristics.

Core Foundation

1. Primary Colors

Colors that cannot be created by mixing other colors together. They are the building blocks of the entire color spectrum.

Red
Yellow
Blue
In Paint: Red, Yellow, Blue (RYB) In Light: Red, Green, Blue (RGB)
Mixed Duos

2. Secondary Colors

Formed by mixing equal parts of two primary colors. They bridge the spectrum and introduce balance.

Orange
Green
Purple
Red + Yellow = Orange Yellow + Blue = Green Blue + Red = Purple

3. Tertiary Colors

Created by mixing a primary color with an adjacent secondary color. They carry hyphenated compound names.

Yellow-Orange
Yellow-Green
Blue-Green
Amber, Vermilion, Magenta, Teal, Chartreuse, Violet
Color Temperature

4. Warm vs. Cool Colors

Color temperature influences spatial perception and mood. Warm colors advance toward the viewer, while cool colors recede.

Warm (Active & Inviting)

Reds, Oranges, Yellows. Evoke sunlight, fire, passion, warmth, and appetite.

Cool (Calm & Expansive)

Blues, Greens, Violets. Evoke water, sky, tranquility, focus, and serenity.

Value Variations

5. Tints, Shades, & Tones

Every pure hue can be manipulated along three distinct dimensions of light and purity:

Tint Pure Hue + White Creates soft, luminous, light pastel variations.
Shade Pure Hue + Black Deepens the hue into rich, dramatic, dark notes.
Tone Pure Hue + Grey Desaturates the hue into sophisticated, muted tones.
Style Families

6. Aesthetic Color Families

Stylistic groupings widely used in design, fashion, architecture, and visual arts:

  • Pastels: High lightness, low saturation (Lavender, Baby Blue, Mint, Peach)
  • Neons: Ultra-saturated, high-luminance, electric (Cyber Green, Hot Pink)
  • Earth Tones: Mineral & soil pigments (Ochre, Terracotta, Umber, Moss)
  • Jewel Tones: Deep, richly saturated gemstone colors (Emerald, Sapphire, Ruby)
  • Neutrals: Low chroma anchors that lack strong hue (Charcoal, Beige, Ivory, Slate)
Science & Computation

Color Models & Digital Color Spaces

Computers, screens, and printers interpret colors through mathematical models. Understand the difference between RGB, CMYK, HSL, and HEX.

Additive (Light)

RGB (Red, Green, Blue)

The model for all digital screens (monitors, phones, TVs). It works by projecting colored beams of light. Combining full Red + Green + Blue produces Pure White.

Channel Ranges: 0 – 255 Total Gamut: 16.7 Million Colors (24-bit)
Subtractive (Pigment)

CMYK (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Key/Black)

The universal standard for physical printing and ink. Pigments absorb light wavelengths. Combining Cyan + Magenta + Yellow creates a dark muddy brown; pure Black (Key) ink is added for crisp depth.

Channel Ranges: 0% – 100% Ideal For: Print, Packaging, Offset Lithography
Perceptual (Human-Intuitive)

HSL (Hue, Saturation, Lightness)

Designed to align with how human beings perceive color. Hue is the angle on the 360° color wheel; Saturation is the vibrancy (0% gray to 100% vivid); Lightness is illumination (0% black to 100% white).

Hue: 0° – 360° Saturation: 0% – 100% • Lightness: 0% – 100%
Web Hexadecimal

HEX Codes (#RRGGBB)

A 6-digit hexadecimal shorthand for 24-bit RGB values. Each pair of digits (00 to FF) represents the intensity of Red, Green, and Blue from 0 to 255.

Format: #RRGGBB Example: #FF5733 = R: 255, G: 87, B: 51
